Googleyness & Leadership — Interview Prep
1

What Is the Googleyness Round?

This is a 45-minute behavioral interview conducted by a senior engineer (not HR). It is one of the four interview loops at Google and carries equal weight to the coding rounds.

  • What it evaluates: collaboration, humility, handling ambiguity, leadership without authority, inclusiveness, and the ability to push back respectfully.
  • What it is NOT: a personality test. You do not need to be bubbly or "Googley" in personality. It is about whether you can work effectively with others to ship great software.
  • Who interviews you: typically a senior engineer or tech lead. They are assessing whether they would want you on their team.
  • Format: 5-7 behavioral questions. Each answer should be 3-5 minutes. The interviewer will ask follow-up questions to dig into specifics.
2

Google's Evaluation Signals

Googleyness

Doing the right thing even when it is hard. Comfort with ambiguity and imperfect information. Genuine collaboration over individual heroics. Intellectual humility — admitting what you do not know. Continuous learning and curiosity about technology and people.

Leadership

Influencing without authority — convincing peers through data and empathy, not title. Mentoring and growing others. Taking ownership of problems nobody asked you to solve. Driving initiatives from idea to shipped product.

Navigating Ambiguity

Making progress with incomplete information rather than waiting for perfect specs. Breaking down unclear problems into actionable steps. Iterating quickly and adjusting course based on feedback. Comfortable saying "I don't know yet, but here is how I will find out."

Communication

Explaining your reasoning clearly and concisely. Active listening — incorporating what the interviewer says into your answers. Structuring your stories so they are easy to follow. Acknowledging trade-offs and opposing viewpoints.

3

The STAR Method

Every behavioral answer should follow the STAR format. This keeps your answers structured, concise, and easy for the interviewer to score.

S

Situation
Set the scene briefly. Company, team, project. ~10% of your time.

T

Task
Your specific responsibility. What was at stake? ~10% of your time.

A

Action
What YOU did. Be specific. Use "I", not "we". ~60% of your time.

R

Result
Quantify impact. Metrics, outcomes, learnings. ~20% of your time.

  • Keep Situation and Task short — do not spend more than 30 seconds setting context.
  • The Action section is where your score comes from. Be detailed and personal: "I analyzed the crash logs and identified three root causes" not "we looked into it".
  • Results should have metrics wherever possible (percentages, counts, ratings).
  • Always end with what you learned or what you would do differently.

Your Stories — 8 STAR Answers

Story 1 — Mentoring & Growing Others

Helping an Engineer Level Up on Compose

"Tell me about a time you helped someone grow."
SITUATION

At DevRev, I had built the internal Jetpack Compose component library (buttons, inputs, bottom sheets, lists) that the entire mobile org depended on. Six engineers on the team were transitioning from XML-based UI to Compose, and one junior engineer in particular was struggling with Compose's state management model — hoisting state, handling recomposition, and using Flows to drive UI.

TASK

As the most experienced Compose developer on the team, I took responsibility for getting this engineer productive. They were blocked on a feature involving a multi-step form with complex validation, and the deadline was two weeks out.

ACTION

I did not just fix their code. Instead, I set up three targeted pair-programming sessions spread over a week. In the first session, I walked through how Compose state hoisting works by refactoring one of our simpler library components together. I explained the mental model: composables as functions of state, not holders of state.

In the second session, we tackled their actual feature. I asked them to drive while I asked guiding questions: "Where should this state live? What happens if the user rotates here?" By the third session, they were making correct architectural decisions independently.

I also wrote an internal Confluence doc titled "Compose State Patterns at DevRev" covering the five most common patterns we used, with code examples pulled from our actual codebase. This became the onboarding reference for the whole team.

RESULT

The engineer shipped the multi-step form feature on time. Within two months, they were leading their own Compose feature independently and even contributed two new components back to the shared library. The documentation I wrote reduced onboarding ramp-up time for the next three hires. I learned that teaching through questions is far more effective than giving answers — it builds lasting understanding, not dependency.

Customize this: Replace with your actual experience with one of the 6 engineers. Which engineer? What specific feature were they building? What specific mistake were they making with state? What was the moment you saw them "get it"?
Story 2 — Taking Initiative / Driving Impact

Cutting Crash Rate 40% Without Being Asked

"Tell me about a time you identified a problem and fixed it without being asked."
SITUATION

At DevRev, I noticed our Android app's crash rate had crept up significantly over the previous quarter. The team was focused on feature delivery, and nobody had been assigned to investigate stability. But I kept seeing crash spikes in Firebase Crashlytics during my morning checks.

TASK

I decided to take ownership of the stability problem even though it was not on any sprint board. I needed to diagnose the root causes, propose a fix, get buy-in from my manager, and execute without disrupting feature work.

ACTION

I spent two evenings analyzing Crashlytics data. I categorized the top crash signatures and found that roughly 60% of crashes came from the network layer — unhandled exceptions during JSON parsing when the API returned unexpected responses, and race conditions in the WebSocket reconnection logic.

I put together a one-page proposal: the root causes, the estimated impact, and a two-week plan to redesign the network error handling. I presented it to my manager with data showing that our crash-free rate was below the Play Store threshold for featuring. That got immediate buy-in.

I redesigned the network layer with proper sealed Result types, added defensive parsing with default values for optional fields, and rebuilt the WebSocket reconnection with exponential backoff and coroutine-scoped lifecycle management. I also reduced the payload size by 35% by switching to a more efficient serialization format and removing redundant fields.

RESULT

Crash rate dropped 40% within three weeks of the rollout. Payload size reduced 35%, which improved load times on slower networks. Our crash-free rate went above 99.5%, making the app eligible for Play Store featuring again. The lesson: monitoring is not just a devops thing — as a mobile engineer, I should be looking at production health proactively.

Customize this: Add the actual crash types you found (NullPointerException? IllegalStateException? Serialization failures?). How exactly did you convince your manager — was it a Slack message, a doc, a meeting? What was the specific serialization change?
Story 3 — Navigating Ambiguity

Designing an SDK With No Clear Spec

"Tell me about a time you had to make progress with unclear requirements."
SITUATION

At DevRev, I was tasked with building Kotlin SDK modules that would wrap our REST and WebSocket APIs for enterprise mobile clients. The challenge: we had 50+ enterprise clients with different integration needs, different tech stacks, and different expectations. There was no product spec — just a mandate to "make it easy for clients to integrate."

TASK

I needed to design an API surface that was flexible enough for diverse client needs but simple enough that integration would not require hand-holding from our team. I had to make architectural decisions with incomplete information about future use cases.

ACTION

Instead of guessing, I started by talking to people. I interviewed five integration engineers from our largest clients to understand their pain points with our existing API. I also sat with our customer success team to understand the most common support tickets.

From these conversations, I identified three conflicting requirements: some clients wanted a high-level "just works" API, others wanted granular control over caching and retry behavior, and a third group needed raw access to the underlying WebSocket for custom event handling.

I designed a layered architecture: a high-level convenience layer on top, a mid-level coroutine-first Flow-based layer for most use cases, and escape hatches to the raw API for power users. I shipped a minimal first version to three beta clients, collected feedback over two weeks, iterated on the API naming and default behaviors, then rolled out broadly.

RESULT

The SDK was adopted by all 50+ enterprise clients. The layered design meant that 80% of clients used the high-level API and integrated in under a day, while power users had the flexibility they needed. Support tickets related to integration dropped significantly. I learned that when requirements are ambiguous, talk to users first and ship small before designing big.

Customize this: Add specific examples of conflicting client requirements. What were the actual API methods? Which beta clients gave you the most useful feedback? What specific API change did you make based on feedback?
Story 4 — Disagreement / Pushing Back

Advocating for Compose Migration at Fiorano

"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate or manager."
SITUATION

At Fiorano, our Android app was built entirely in XML layouts. The codebase had accumulated significant UI tech debt — duplicate layout files, inconsistent styling, and brittle custom views. Our Play Store rating was 3.8, partly due to UI jank and inconsistencies.

TASK

I proposed migrating to Jetpack Compose, but a senior teammate pushed back. Their argument: Compose was too new, the team did not have experience with it, and migrating would risk the stability of a production app used by enterprise customers. They wanted to keep XML and just refactor the existing layouts.

ACTION

I did not dismiss their concerns — they were valid. Instead, I built a case with data. I spent a weekend prototyping one of our most complex screens in both refactored XML and Compose. I documented: lines of code (40% less in Compose), build time (faster incremental builds with Compose), and the number of layout bugs that would be structurally impossible in Compose (state-driven UI eliminates an entire class of bugs).

I presented this to the team and proposed a compromise: an incremental migration strategy. We would start with new screens only, using ComposeView inside existing XML Activities. No rewrite of existing screens. If the team felt Compose was not working after one month, we would stop.

I also volunteered to write the Compose coding guidelines and review all initial Compose PRs to maintain quality during the transition.

RESULT

The team agreed to the incremental approach. Over the next year, we shipped 15+ releases with Compose screens. Developer velocity improved — new screens took roughly half the time. Test coverage rose from 40% to 72% because Compose's testable architecture made it easier to write UI tests. The Play Store rating improved from 3.8 to 4.5. The key learning: disagreements are productive when you bring data, propose a low-risk path, and address the other person's concerns head-on.

Customize this: Replace with your actual disagreement — was it with a specific person? What was their exact concern? What data point was most convincing? Could also be about architecture, priorities, or timeline rather than Compose specifically.
Story 5 — Handling Failure

DETE: When the Tech Worked but the Business Did Not

"Tell me about a time something didn't go as planned."
SITUATION

I co-founded DETE, a local delivery platform, during college. I architected three Kotlin apps (User, Merchant, Driver) with shared modules, built a realtime layer over Firebase with Flow-based APIs, and led a 15-member team. We scaled to 500+ users and 60+ merchants.

TASK

As co-founder and lead engineer, I was responsible for both the technology and, ultimately, the business outcomes. We needed to grow from a college-town operation to a sustainable business.

ACTION

On the technology side, things went well. I built a clean modular architecture, the realtime delivery tracking worked reliably, and the apps were stable. But I made a mistake: I spent too much time perfecting the technology and too little time validating the business model.

I was focused on building reusable shared modules, optimizing Firebase queries, and making the architecture "right" — when the real problem was that our unit economics did not work at our scale. Delivery costs in a small city were too high relative to order values.

When I realized this, I tried to pivot — we explored different pricing models and expanded our merchant base. But we had burned too much runway on engineering and not enough on market validation.

RESULT

DETE did not scale into a sustainable business. But the technical and personal lessons were significant. I learned that engineering excellence without product-market fit is wasted effort. I learned to validate assumptions early before building infrastructure. This experience fundamentally changed how I approach projects — at DevRev and Fiorano, I always ask "what is the user impact?" before investing in technical work. It also gave me deep empathy for startup founders and product managers who are making difficult prioritization calls every day.

Customize this: Add the actual reason DETE did not continue — was it funding, market fit, team, competition? Be honest and specific. Google values self-awareness. What specific moment made you realize things were not working?
Story 6 — Collaboration Across Teams

Working with Enterprise Clients on SDK Adoption

"Tell me about a time you worked with people outside your team."
SITUATION

At DevRev, the Kotlin SDK I built needed to be adopted by 50+ enterprise clients who had their own engineering teams, their own architectures, and their own timelines. The SDK was not just a code artifact — it needed documentation, migration guides, and hands-on support.

TASK

I needed to collaborate with customer success, developer relations, and the clients' own engineering teams to drive adoption. This meant working with non-engineers and external stakeholders — a very different dynamic from my day-to-day mobile team work.

ACTION

I started by creating comprehensive migration guides with step-by-step instructions and sample projects. But I quickly learned that documentation alone was not enough — different clients had different levels of Android expertise.

I worked with the customer success team to identify the top 10 clients by revenue and set up direct Slack channels with their mobile engineers. For the three most complex integrations, I did live code-review sessions over video calls, walking through their codebases and showing exactly where our SDK plugged in.

I also set up a feedback loop: after each integration, I asked the client's engineer to fill out a short survey on what was confusing. I compiled this feedback and used it to improve both the SDK's API design and the documentation. For example, one client's feedback led me to add default timeout configurations and a simpler initialization flow.

RESULT

All 50+ clients successfully integrated the SDK. Average integration time dropped from what customer success estimated at a week to under two days. The feedback loop became a standard practice at DevRev for all SDK launches. I learned that collaboration across teams requires speaking the other person's language — customer success thinks in terms of time-to-value, clients think in terms of risk, and engineers think in terms of API cleanliness.

Story 7 — Going Above and Beyond

Raising Test Coverage from 40% to 72%

"Tell me about a time you went beyond your job description."
SITUATION

At Fiorano, our Android app had 40% test coverage. Most of it was basic happy-path unit tests. There were no integration tests, no UI tests, and no CI enforcement. Bugs were caught in QA or, worse, by customers. On the side, I had also been building RouteChef, an offline-first travel app that eventually reached 50K+ downloads.

TASK

Improving test coverage was not part of my assigned work — I was hired to build features. But I believed that the team's velocity was suffering because of the constant regressions. I decided to make testing a priority on my own initiative.

ACTION

I started by leading by example. For every feature I built, I wrote comprehensive tests — unit tests for business logic, integration tests for repository layers with in-memory Room databases, and Compose UI tests for critical screens. I made sure my PRs always included tests, and I explained my testing approach in PR descriptions.

I then proposed a team-wide testing initiative: a two-week sprint where each engineer would add tests to their most recent feature. I created a shared testing guide with patterns specific to our codebase (how to test our ViewModel pattern, how to mock our API layer, how to use Compose test rules). I also configured CI to run tests on every PR and added a coverage threshold that would block merges below 60%.

For RouteChef, I applied the same discipline — rigorous testing helped me reduce the crash rate from 2.5% to 0.8% despite working on it in evenings and weekends.

RESULT

Fiorano's test coverage rose from 40% to 72%. More importantly, the regression rate in QA dropped noticeably, which meant faster release cycles. RouteChef hit 50K+ downloads with a 0.8% crash rate, proving that quality and side-project pace can coexist. I learned that going above and beyond is not about working more hours — it is about investing time in things that create leverage for the whole team.

Customize this: Pick the story that resonates more — Fiorano testing or RouteChef. If Fiorano: what specific regression motivated you? If RouteChef: what drove you to build it alongside a full-time job? Combine them or focus on one.
Story 8 — Leading Without Authority

Leading 15 Engineers at DETE as Co-Founder

"Tell me about a time you led a project or initiative without being the manager."
SITUATION

At DETE, I was the technical co-founder, not a traditional engineering manager. Our team of 15 included college students, freelancers, and part-time contributors — none of whom reported to me formally. We were building three interconnected Kotlin apps (User, Merchant, Driver) simultaneously, and everyone had different skill levels and availability.

TASK

I needed to keep 15 people aligned on architecture decisions, code quality standards, and release timelines — without any formal authority, performance review leverage, or even a salary to offer most of them.

ACTION

I led through three strategies: shared ownership, technical credibility, and genuine care.

First, I designed a shared module architecture where common code (networking, auth, models) lived in a shared Kotlin module. This forced collaboration — when someone improved the shared module, everyone benefited, which created natural buy-in.

Second, I established credibility by being the hardest worker. I wrote the initial architecture, built the Firebase realtime layer, set up CI, and wrote the coding standards. When I asked someone to follow a convention, they knew it was not arbitrary — I had done the work to validate it.

Third, I invested in each person's growth. I matched tasks to skill levels, gave detailed PR reviews that taught rather than just corrected, and publicly credited people's contributions in our weekly standup. When a team member wanted to quit because they felt their work on the Driver app was less visible, I restructured the demo to showcase all three apps equally and gave them ownership of a user-facing feature in the main app.

RESULT

We shipped all three apps, scaled to 500+ users and 60+ merchants, and maintained team cohesion for over a year — remarkable for a volunteer student team. Two team members went on to get Android roles at companies, citing DETE as their primary experience. I learned that leadership without authority comes down to three things: earn respect through your own work, give people ownership, and genuinely care about their growth.

Customize this: Add a specific moment where you had to convince a team member to change direction. What was the disagreement? How did you handle it without being able to "just decide"? Who was the person who almost quit, and what specifically brought them back?

You want to be in an environment where these practices are the norm, not something you have to advocate for.

Day-Of Tips

💬

Think out loud. If you need a moment to recall a story, say "Let me think about the best example for this." Silence feels longer to the interviewer than it does to you.

❓

Ask clarifying questions. "Are you looking for a technical disagreement or an interpersonal one?" shows maturity and buys you time to pick the right story.

👤

Use "I" not "we." The interviewer needs to evaluate YOUR contribution. "I proposed the migration strategy" not "we decided to migrate."

⏸

Pause and think. It is completely OK to take 5-10 seconds before answering. A thoughtful answer beats a rushed one every time.

🔍

Show self-awareness. For every story, know what you would do differently. "If I were to do it again, I would start with user interviews even earlier."

🎯

End with the learning. Every STAR answer should close with what you learned or how it changed your approach. This signals growth mindset.

🙌

Be genuine. Rehearsed answers sound fake. Know your stories well enough that you can tell them naturally, not recite them word-for-word.

🔄

Pivot gracefully. If you do not have a perfect story, say: "I have not faced that exact situation, but the closest experience is..." Honesty is better than fabrication.

Red Flags to Avoid

Blaming others. Even if someone else made a mistake, focus on what YOU did to resolve it. "The PM gave us bad requirements" is a red flag. "I realized the requirements were unclear so I set up a meeting to align" is a green flag.

Taking all the credit. On team projects, acknowledge others' contributions. Then clearly articulate YOUR specific part. "The team shipped the feature, and my specific contribution was designing the SDK architecture and writing the migration guides."

Having no failures. If you cannot share a failure, the interviewer assumes you either have not done challenging work or lack self-awareness. DETE is a strong, honest failure story. Use it.

Generic answers without specifics. "I am a team player" means nothing. "I set up weekly 1:1 code review sessions with a junior engineer and they shipped their first independent feature within two months" is a signal.

Saying "we" for everything. The interviewer cannot assess what you did if every sentence starts with "we." Use "I" for your actions, "we" only when describing team outcomes.

Badmouthing previous employers. Even if a previous job was terrible, frame it constructively. "The process was not ideal, which motivated me to propose improvements" not "management was incompetent."

Not showing growth. Every story should end with a learning. If you tell the same story from 2022 with no evolution in your thinking, it suggests stagnation. Show how past experiences shaped your current approach.
