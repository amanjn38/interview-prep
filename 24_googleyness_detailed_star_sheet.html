S1 — Mentoring Sahil (intern → owner)

Hook: I ramped an intern from zero Android to owning a core SDK path without trading away my own velocity or our quality bar.

Situation & task: At DevRev I owned parts of our mobile SDK. Sahil joined as an intern straight out of college with no Android experience. His early PRs needed many rounds, and I was already carrying review load for a sensitive area. My job was both to ship and to grow him — not to do his work for him.

Actions: I started pair programming on tiny, scoped tasks so he could see how I reasoned about lifecycle and state. Early on I caught myself giving line-level fixes; I deliberately shifted to guiding questions — for example where state should live and what would break on rotation — so he had to explain his model before I approved scope increases. I reviewed approach before nitpicking diffs, which cost me more time up front but cut churn later. I only expanded his tasks after he could defend trade-offs. When he first pushed back on one of my suggestions with a better alternative, I treated that as the real success signal, not just merged PRs.

Result: Within about two months he independently owned wireframe traversal for session replay — a core SDK capability. Review cycles became shorter and his PRs needed fewer structural rewrites.

Trade-off: Coaching this way is slower than fixing files yourself short-term; I chose sustainable ownership over short-term speed.

Learning: Structured autonomy with a rising bar beats both hand-holding and sink-or-swim.

S2 — Crash rate / production health (self-initiated)

Hook: I drove a large crash reduction nobody had slotted — because stability was slipping and users were paying for it.

Situation & task: Crash metrics were trending the wrong way while the team stayed focused on feature work. There was no named owner for a deep investigation. I treated it like a product risk: quantify root cause, propose a tight plan, execute, and prove impact — not random patches.

Actions: I spent two focused evenings in Crashlytics and found roughly 60% of signal clustering around the network path — brittle JSON handling, WebSocket reconnect races, and lifecycle gaps. I wrote a one-pager with impact, proposed sequencing, and tied it to Play Store featuring risk so the decision was business-visible, not “Aman’s side quest.” After buy-in I redesigned that layer with sealed Result types, defensive parsing, exponential backoff on reconnect, and coroutine scopes tied to lifecycle so failures stopped compounding. I also reduced payload size materially by changing serialization where safe.

Result: Crash rate dropped on the order of ~40%; crash-free sessions moved above ~99.5%; we regained eligibility confidence for featuring. Fewer emergency releases.

Trade-off: I temporarily borrowed nights from roadmap work — I made that explicit to my manager when negotiating priority.

Learning: Production monitoring is part of the craft for mobile engineers, not a separate team’s problem.

S3 — SDK with no spec (50+ clients, ambiguity)

Hook: The spec was effectively “make integration easy” — I turned conflicting needs into a layered platform instead of a one-size-fits-none compromise.

Situation & task: I built Kotlin SDK modules wrapping REST and WebSocket APIs for dozens of enterprise clients. Requirements conflicted: some wanted a high-level “just works” surface, others needed granular control, and a few needed raw access. Picking one persona would have forked support load or forced bad abstractions.

Actions: I interviewed five integration engineers from large accounts and paired with customer success to see real tickets. I mapped three patterns, then designed a layered API: convenience defaults, a Flow-based mid layer for streaming cases, and explicit escape hatches for power users. I shipped a thin vertical to three beta clients, measured which tier they used, and iterated before broad rollout.

Result: About 80% of clients stayed on the high-level path; integrations commonly landed under a day; fewer bespoke threads for edge cases.

Trade-off: Layering is more code to maintain than a single facade; I accepted that cost to avoid N custom forks.

Learning: In ambiguity, talk to users first and ship small before designing the whole universe.

S3B — Session replay rebuild (contractor exit, no docs)

Hook: I inherited a stalled, fragile replay pipeline and chose a controlled rebuild once one real user path made the failure mode obvious.

Situation & task: Legacy Java replay code had OOM risk from bitmap handling, races from unstructured concurrency, NPEs in traversal, and callback-heavy pipelines that were hard to test. A contractor rewrite ran long; they were stretched across contracts and left without docs. Product still needed a reliable path to ship.

Actions: Before exit I ran weekly syncs and read their modules to reduce single-person dependency. After they left I traced one screenshot end-to-end: capture → zip → upload → backend reconstruction. Salvage would have meant patching race classes indefinitely, so I rebuilt the capture/upload slice in Kotlin with structured concurrency, Flow with backpressure, sealed upload states, and vertical slices — one path working before edge-case sprawl.

Result: We moved from “stuck for months” to shipping; eliminated whole classes of crashes tied to the old approach.

Trade-off: Rewrite risk vs. patch risk — I chose rewrite only after the trace proved depth of coupling.

Learning: Never assume handoff; always own one path e2e early. With difficult collaborators, reduce dependency and document decisions.
